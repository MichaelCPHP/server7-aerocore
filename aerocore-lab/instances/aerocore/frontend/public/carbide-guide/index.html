How to Identify Tungsten Carbide

A practical guide for machine shops and manufacturers to identify carbide scrap and understand what affects its value.

Material Overview

What is Tungsten Carbide?

Tungsten carbide (WC) is an extremely hard compound made from tungsten and carbon atoms, typically bonded together with a cobalt (Co) binder. With a hardness approaching that of diamond on the Mohs scale (approximately 9 to 9.5), tungsten carbide is one of the hardest commercially available materials in the world. It is roughly twice as dense as steel, giving it a distinctive heft that experienced machinists recognize immediately.

Tungsten carbide is widely used in precision cutting tools such as end mills, inserts, drill bits, and reamers, as well as wear parts, mining tools, and dies. Because of the high value of tungsten and cobalt, scrap carbide commands strong prices in the recycling market, making it well worth identifying and separating from other shop scrap.

Identification

4 Quick Tests to Identify Carbide

You do not need special equipment to tell carbide apart from steel. These four simple tests can be performed right on the shop floor.

1. Weight Test

Tungsten carbide is approximately 2.5 times heavier than steel. Pick up a piece of suspected carbide — it will feel surprisingly heavy for its size. A solid carbide end mill weighs noticeably more than an identical-sized HSS tool. This is the fastest and most reliable shop-floor test.

2. Hardness Test

Carbide scratches glass easily without leaving any damage to itself. Press the edge of the suspected piece firmly against a piece of glass and drag it — carbide will score a clean scratch line, while steel will typically slide or leave only a faint mark. Carbide also cannot be scratched with a file.

3. Magnet Test

Tungsten carbide is not magnetic. Hold a magnet to the material — if it does not attract, it is likely carbide. Note that some carbide grades with higher cobalt content may show a very slight magnetic attraction, but it will be far weaker than the strong pull you would feel with steel or HSS.

4. Spark Test

Touch the piece to a grinding wheel and observe the sparks. Carbide produces very few, small, dark orange sparks that die quickly. Steel, in contrast, throws off a large shower of bright white or yellow sparks with branching patterns. This visual difference is unmistakable once you have seen it side by side.

Forms & Types

Common Forms of Carbide Scrap

Carbide scrap comes from a variety of sources in machining and manufacturing operations. Knowing what to look for helps you collect and separate material for maximum value.

  • End Mills — Solid carbide end mills are the most common form of carbide scrap in machine shops. Used, broken, or worn-out end mills are highly valuable.
  • Inserts — Indexable carbide inserts come in many shapes (CNMG, TNMG, VNMG, etc.) and are used in turning, milling, and boring operations. Sort by grade for best pricing.
  • Drill Bits — Solid carbide drills and carbide-tipped drills are both recyclable. Solid carbide drills command the highest prices.
  • Bars & Rounds — Unground carbide blanks, rods, and round stock are premium scrap since they contain no coatings and are typically high-grade material.
  • Wear Parts — Carbide wear plates, bushings, seats, and nozzles used in industrial equipment are recyclable and often made from high-cobalt grades.
  • Tips (Brazed) — Carbide tips brazed onto steel shanks (such as saw tips or road-planing bits) have lower per-pound value since the steel must be separated, but they are still worth collecting.
  • Sludge & Swarf — Grinding sludge and machining swarf containing carbide particles can be recycled. This material must be dried and is priced based on carbide content.
Comparison

Carbide vs Steel — How to Tell the Difference

The most common confusion in shop scrap bins is between tungsten carbide and high-speed steel (HSS) tools, since both are used for cutting operations and can look similar. The key differences are weight, magnetism, and sparks. Carbide is roughly 2.5 times heavier than steel and is not magnetic, while HSS is strongly attracted to a magnet. On a grinding wheel, carbide produces a few dim orange sparks, while HSS throws a dramatic shower of bright white sparks. Carbide tools also tend to have a darker, more uniform gray color compared to the slightly lighter, sometimes bluish finish of HSS. When in doubt, the weight test is the most reliable — if a tool feels noticeably heavier than it looks like it should, it is almost certainly carbide.

Grades

Carbide Grades and Composition

Tungsten carbide grades are classified on a scale from C1 through C14, established by the U.S. industry. Lower C-numbers (C1-C4) are designed for wear resistance and typically contain less cobalt (3-6%), making them harder and more brittle. Higher C-numbers (C5-C8) are engineered for cutting applications and contain more cobalt (6-12%), which provides greater toughness for absorbing the impact of machining operations. Specialty grades (C9-C14) are tailored for specific applications such as mining, construction, and high-impact tooling.

The cobalt percentage directly affects both the performance characteristics and the scrap value of carbide. Higher cobalt content generally means higher scrap value because cobalt is a valuable metal in its own right. When selling carbide scrap, knowing the grade or at least the approximate cobalt percentage can help you get a more accurate quote. If the grade is unknown, buyers like AeroCore evaluate the material through testing to determine its composition and provide a fair market price.

Value Factors

What Affects Carbide Scrap Value?

Several factors determine how much your carbide scrap is worth on the current market:

  • Grade — Higher-grade carbide with known compositions commands premium prices. C2 and C6 grades are among the most common and most valuable.
  • Condition — Solid, clean carbide is worth more than material mixed with steel, solder, or other contaminants. Sorting and cleaning your scrap before selling can significantly increase its value.
  • Quantity — Larger lots (50+ lbs) typically qualify for volume bonuses. However, AeroCore accepts any quantity, from a handful of end mills to full-pallet shipments.
  • Cobalt Content — Cobalt is a critical raw material used in batteries, superalloys, and other high-tech applications. Higher cobalt content in the carbide binder increases scrap value.
  • Coatings — TiN, TiAlN, AlCrN, and other coatings do not significantly reduce value, but uncoated carbide is marginally easier to recycle and may receive a slight premium.
